The Soviet Union: A Superpower

The Soviet Union, once a global superpower, shaped world events and the course of history. Explore its power, influence, and lasting impact on global geopolitics.

About the Soviet Union

The Soviet Union, or the Union of Soviet Socialist Republics (USSR), was a federation of socialist republics governed by the Communist Party. It played a pivotal role in shaping the 20th century and had a profound impact on global politics, economics, and military affairs.

The Rise of the Soviet Union

The Soviet Union emerged after the Russian Revolution of 1917, following the overthrow of the Russian monarchy. Led by the Communist Party under Vladimir Lenin, the USSR quickly became a global superpower, challenging capitalist democracies and shaping world events for much of the 20th century.

Through industrialization, the development of a powerful military, and strategic alliances, the Soviet Union became a key player in the Cold War, battling the United States for influence and dominance.

Political Structure of the Soviet Union

The Soviet Union was a union of multiple socialist republics, with its capital in Moscow. It was governed by a single-party system, the Communist Party of the Soviet Union (CPSU). This centralized structure created a powerful, but tightly controlled, government that directed economic and military strategies across the entire federation.

The highest executive body in the Soviet Union was the Supreme Soviet, which was responsible for creating laws and making decisions about foreign policy, military strategy, and national development. The General Secretary of the Communist Party, such as Joseph Stalin, held immense power and influence over the direction of the state.

Despite its centralization, the Soviet Union’s leadership was not without its internal struggles, including purges, power struggles, and eventual collapse in 1991. However, during its prime, it was a force to be reckoned with on the global stage.

Military Power of the Soviet Union

One of the pillars of Soviet strength was its military power. The Soviet Red Army was considered one of the largest and most formidable military forces in the world. The USSR invested heavily in military technology, from the development of nuclear weapons to advanced aircraft and tanks.

The Cold War was, in many ways, a military contest between the Soviet Union and the United States. Both superpowers had massive stockpiles of nuclear weapons, and the Soviet Union also invested heavily in its conventional forces to maintain its global influence. The USSR played a key role in key conflicts, such as World War II and the Korean War, and was involved in proxy wars across the world, including in Afghanistan and Vietnam.

Military superiority was a major factor in the USSR’s rivalry with the West, but also led to the eventual collapse as the Soviet economy became overburdened by its military spending.

The Soviet Union and the Space Race

The Soviet Union was a key player in the Space Race, competing directly with the United States to be the first to explore space. The USSR's early space achievements were monumental, and it achieved significant firsts in space exploration.

In 1957, the Soviet Union launched the first artificial satellite, Sputnik 1, into orbit. This event marked the beginning of the Space Age and sent shockwaves throughout the world, demonstrating Soviet technological superiority. Following Sputnik, the USSR continued to push the boundaries of space exploration, sending the first human, Yuri Gagarin, into space in 1961 aboard Vostok 1.

The achievements of the Soviet space program not only sparked global interest in space exploration but also increased tensions in the Cold War as both the USSR and the United States sought to establish dominance in space. The legacy of the Soviet space program continues to inspire many spacefaring nations today.

Cultural Influence of the Soviet Union

The Soviet Union was not only a political and military powerhouse but also a significant cultural force. Despite its authoritarian regime, the Soviet Union produced some of the most iconic artists, writers, musicians, and filmmakers of the 20th century.

Soviet culture emphasized socialist realism, which portrayed the working class and the state as powerful and heroic. This art style was heavily supported by the state, and artists were expected to create works that advanced the Communist ideology. Iconic Soviet filmmakers such as Andrei Tarkovsky created revolutionary cinematic works that continue to be studied and admired worldwide.

Music and literature also flourished, with composers like Dmitri Shostakovich and writers like Aleksandr Solzhenitsyn becoming internationally recognized. Despite the strict censorship imposed by the government, the Soviet Union’s cultural output had a lasting impact on the global arts scene.

The Soviet Economy

The economy of the Soviet Union was based on central planning, where the government controlled all major industries and set production targets for the entire economy. The goal was to create a society in which the government ensured full employment, rapid industrialization, and the provision of basic goods to all citizens.

While the Soviet Union experienced rapid growth in the early decades, particularly during the industrialization efforts under Joseph Stalin, its economy eventually faltered. The lack of competition and inefficiency in the centrally planned economy led to stagnation in the later years of the USSR. Furthermore, the emphasis on heavy industry at the expense of consumer goods meant that citizens often had limited access to basic goods.

In the 1980s, under Mikhail Gorbachev, the Soviet Union began reforms like Glasnost (openness) and Perestroika (restructuring), but these reforms were too little, too late to save the crumbling Soviet economy. The fall of the Soviet Union in 1991 was, in part, due to the inability of its economic system to adapt to the challenges of the modern world.

International Relations and Cold War Politics

The Soviet Union's foreign policy was heavily influenced by its ideological battle with the United States and the capitalist West. The USSR sought to spread communism across the world, supporting revolutionary movements in Africa, Asia, and Latin America. This often led to proxy wars and military interventions, such as the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan in 1979.

The Cold War was defined by an arms race, a space race, and various political and military standoffs, such as the Cuban Missile Crisis. The ideological divide between the Soviet Union and the United States resulted in decades of tension and conflict, affecting global politics and shaping the modern world order.

Despite these tensions, the Soviet Union had many allies within the socialist bloc, including Eastern European nations and communist states in Asia. The USSR was the leader of the Warsaw Pact, a military alliance formed to counter NATO, and exerted significant influence over countries within its sphere of influence.

The Legacy of the Soviet Union

Although the Soviet Union dissolved in 1991, its legacy continues to shape global politics, culture, and society. Many of the former Soviet republics, such as Russia, Ukraine, and Belarus, continue to deal with the consequences of the USSR's collapse, including economic struggles, political instability, and national identity issues.

The Soviet Union’s legacy also persists in its cultural contributions, from literature to space exploration to the arts. In Russia and other former Soviet states, there are still debates about the Soviet era, with some people viewing it nostalgically and others criticizing its authoritarianism and human rights abuses.

Despite the fall of the Soviet state, the ideas of communism and socialism continue to resonate in various parts of the world, influencing political movements and ideologies. The Soviet Union’s impact on global history cannot be overstated, and its legacy remains a significant part of the modern world’s geopolitical landscape.

The Cultural Influence of the Soviet Union

The Soviet Union had a profound impact on global culture, shaping the world in numerous ways. The nation produced a wealth of art, literature, music, and cinema that influenced both Eastern and Western cultures. One of the key aspects of Soviet culture was its socialist realism, a style that celebrated the working class and the achievements of the state.

Art and Literature

Soviet art and literature were heavily influenced by state ideology, with many works produced to support socialist ideals. Authors like Maxim Gorky, Isaac Babel, and Alexandr Solzhenitsyn contributed to the rich literary tradition of the Soviet Union, though often facing censorship and political persecution.

Visual arts were similarly influenced by socialist realism, where artists were expected to depict idealized representations of workers, soldiers, and the victories of socialism. Famous Soviet artists included Ilya Repin and Vladimir Favorsky.

Music and Performing Arts

Music played an essential role in the cultural life of the Soviet Union, with the state sponsoring large orchestras and ballet companies. Composers like Dmitri Shostakovich and Sergei Prokofiev are some of the most well-known figures in Soviet music. Soviet ballet, led by famous dancers like Galina Ulanova, became a symbol of Soviet cultural achievement.

Additionally, Soviet film production gained international recognition. Films like Andrei Tarkovsky’s "Solaris" and Sergei Eisenstein’s "Battleship Potemkin" influenced cinematic techniques and visual storytelling worldwide.

Architecture and Design

Architecturally, the Soviet Union left a legacy that is both grand and utilitarian. The government often commissioned monumental structures that symbolized the power of the state, such as the Palace of Soviets in Moscow. The style of Soviet architecture is best known for its monumental and brutalist forms, reflecting the ideals of strength, solidarity, and progress. Famous Soviet architects like Vladimir Shukhov and Alexei Dushkin played vital roles in designing iconic buildings that still stand in Russia today.

Impact on Global Pop Culture

Though the Soviet Union collapsed in 1991, its influence on global culture remains significant. Many artists, musicians, and intellectuals continue to draw inspiration from Soviet history and the culture it created. Artists like Vladimir Tatlin and Kazimir Malevich shaped the abstract art movement, while the state-sponsored art of the Soviet era continues to inspire modern exhibitions.

The Soviet Union's influence is also present in literature, with authors such as George Orwell drawing direct comparisons to totalitarian regimes in novels like "1984" and "Animal Farm." The Cold War rivalry with the United States, often dramatized in movies, also made Soviet figures central to many historical and fictional works worldwide.

The Soviet Union's Economy

The economy of the Soviet Union was characterized by state control over nearly all aspects of production, distribution, and consumption. The government managed the nation's resources through a central planning system, also known as the command economy. This model allowed the state to prioritize sectors such as industry, agriculture, and defense, with the goal of achieving rapid economic growth and development.

Centralized Planning

The Soviet government adopted centralized economic planning through a series of Five-Year Plans, which set ambitious production targets for various industries. While these plans achieved significant industrial growth, particularly in heavy industry such as steel, coal, and machinery, they also led to inefficiencies, shortages, and waste. The economy struggled to meet the needs of its citizens, and consumer goods were often scarce or of poor quality.

The state-controlled economy also faced a lack of innovation due to the suppression of private enterprise and the prioritization of military production over consumer goods. Despite these challenges, the Soviet Union achieved considerable economic power, particularly during the early years of its existence. The nation was a global superpower, with an economy that competed with the United States during the Cold War.

Agriculture and Collectivization

Agriculture in the Soviet Union was characterized by state-owned collective farms and state-run farms, collectively known as kolkhozes and sovkhozes. The collectivization of agriculture in the 1930s aimed to consolidate small farms into large, more efficient collective units that could increase production. However, this policy caused widespread hardship, particularly in Ukraine, where millions died as a result of famine caused by poor harvests and government mismanagement. The forced collectivization was a central part of Joseph Stalin's economic strategy, but it left a legacy of inefficiency and dissatisfaction in rural areas.

Industrialization and the Arms Race

One of the primary goals of the Soviet economy was rapid industrialization. The state poured resources into building heavy industries, military production, and infrastructure, such as railroads, factories, and dams. By the 1960s, the Soviet Union had become a global industrial powerhouse, with significant production in steel, coal, and machinery. However, this industrial focus came at the expense of the consumer economy, leaving Soviet citizens with fewer consumer goods compared to those in the West.

The arms race during the Cold War also significantly shaped the Soviet economy. The military-industrial complex received enormous investment, leading to the development of nuclear weapons, space exploration, and military technology. While this bolstered Soviet prestige and security, it also drained resources away from other critical sectors like agriculture and consumer goods.

Technological Development in the Soviet Union

Although the Soviet Union’s economy faced many difficulties, its scientific and technological achievements were impressive. The country made groundbreaking advancements in space exploration, nuclear energy, and military technology, which became symbols of Soviet strength and innovation.

The Space Race

The Soviet Union was a pioneer in space exploration, achieving numerous significant milestones during the Cold War. In 1957, the Soviets launched the first artificial satellite, Sputnik 1, into orbit, marking the beginning of the Space Age. This was followed by the launch of the first human-made object to orbit the Earth, Sputnik 2, which carried the first living being into space—Laika the dog.

In 1961, Soviet cosmonaut Yuri Gagarin became the first human to travel into space, orbiting the Earth aboard Vostok 1. This achievement was a source of immense pride for the Soviet Union and cemented its place as a world leader in space technology. The space race between the United States and the Soviet Union became one of the defining features of the Cold War, with the Soviet Union making significant strides in space exploration.

Nuclear Power

The Soviet Union was also a major player in the development of nuclear technology. Soviet scientists and engineers played a key role in the development of nuclear energy for both military and civilian purposes. The USSR’s nuclear program included the development of atomic bombs and nuclear submarines, which provided the Soviet military with a strategic advantage during the Cold War. The civilian nuclear power industry also grew, with the Soviets constructing a series of nuclear reactors and power plants to supply energy to the nation.

Military Technology

Military technology was a central focus of Soviet innovation. The Soviet Union developed a wide range of advanced military equipment, including tanks, fighter jets, and ballistic missiles. Some of the most famous Soviet military inventions included the T-34 tank during World War II, and the MiG series of aircraft, which became iconic symbols of Soviet military might.

The Political Structure of the Soviet Union

The political structure of the Soviet Union was based on a centralized one-party system, dominated by the Communist Party. The government functioned under the principle of democratic centralism, with all decisions made by the leadership of the Communist Party and then carried out by the government. The USSR’s political system was designed to embody the principles of Marxism-Leninism, and it placed a significant emphasis on state control over all aspects of life, including the economy, education, and cultural expression.

Leadership and the Role of the Communist Party

At the heart of the Soviet political system was the Communist Party, which controlled every level of government. The party was led by a General Secretary, who held the most powerful position in the country. The role of the General Secretary was pivotal in shaping both domestic and foreign policy. Early leaders such as Vladimir Lenin and Joseph Stalin were central to the formation of the Soviet Union, while later leaders like Leonid Brezhnev and Mikhail Gorbachev played critical roles during the Cold War and the eventual dissolution of the USSR.

The Soviet political system was highly centralized. All significant decisions were made by the Communist Party’s Politburo, which was composed of senior party officials. Below the Politburo were various state bodies that helped administer the country, including the Supreme Soviet (the legislative body) and the Council of Ministers (which acted as the executive branch). Despite the existence of these institutions, real political power was concentrated in the hands of the party elite.

Dictatorship and Totalitarian Control

The Soviet Union was known for its totalitarian nature, with the state exerting control over nearly every aspect of life. The secret police, such as the KGB, played a major role in maintaining order by surveilling citizens, suppressing dissent, and quelling opposition movements. Political repression was widespread, particularly under leaders like Stalin, who purged large numbers of political opponents. Millions were arrested, executed, or sent to labor camps in Siberia during the Great Purge of the 1930s.

The government's ability to control the media and restrict access to information was a cornerstone of Soviet power. The state-run news outlets consistently promoted the achievements of socialism, and all forms of political opposition were silenced. This created a system of widespread fear and conformity, though many citizens still held deep, if quiet, dissatisfaction with the regime.

The Role of the Military

The Soviet military, known as the Red Army, was a central pillar of the Soviet state. The Soviet Union had one of the most powerful military forces in the world, and the military played a vital role in both domestic politics and foreign affairs. The Red Army was instrumental in defeating Nazi Germany during World War II, and it became a key instrument of Soviet influence during the Cold War, particularly in Eastern Europe.

While the military was a powerful tool for the Soviet government, it was also a means of ensuring loyalty to the state. Military service was compulsory for all Soviet men, and the state made sure that the military was firmly under the control of the Communist Party. Generals and officers who were seen as disloyal or untrustworthy were often purged or removed from their positions.

The Collapse of the Soviet Union

The Soviet Union, once a dominant superpower, collapsed in 1991 after a series of political, economic, and social crises. The dissolution of the Soviet Union marked the end of the Cold War and the beginning of a new era in global politics. There were numerous factors that contributed to the collapse of the USSR, including economic stagnation, political corruption, and nationalistic movements within the republics.

Economic Struggles and Reforms

By the 1980s, the Soviet economy was in a state of crisis. The central planning system, which had once propelled the Soviet Union to economic prominence, had become increasingly inefficient. There were shortages of consumer goods, inflation, and a lack of innovation. The state’s focus on military spending and heavy industry left other sectors, such as agriculture and consumer goods, underdeveloped.

In response to the mounting economic challenges, the Soviet leadership, under Mikhail Gorbachev, introduced a series of reforms known as glasnost (openness) and perestroika (restructuring). Glasnost aimed to increase transparency in government and allow greater freedom of speech, while perestroika was an effort to revitalize the economy by allowing some elements of market reforms. However, these reforms had unintended consequences, leading to political instability and a weakening of central control.

Nationalism and Independence Movements

One of the major factors contributing to the Soviet Union's collapse was the rise of nationalism within its constituent republics. As the Soviet economy weakened and political reform spread, many republics began to push for greater autonomy or full independence. In 1991, the Baltic republics of Lithuania, Latvia, and Estonia declared their independence, and other regions followed suit. These nationalist movements were a direct challenge to Soviet unity and authority.

In addition to the Baltic republics, other regions, including Ukraine, Georgia, and Armenia, also sought independence from the Soviet Union. This fragmentation of the Soviet state contributed to its eventual collapse, as the central government in Moscow was unable to control the growing calls for independence.

The August Coup and the Fall of Gorbachev

In August 1991, a group of hardline Communist Party officials attempted a coup to oust Gorbachev and reverse his reforms. The coup failed, but it accelerated the disintegration of the Soviet Union. In the aftermath of the coup, Gorbachev’s power was greatly diminished, and the republics began to formally declare their independence. On December 26, 1991, the Soviet Union was officially dissolved, and the Russian Federation, led by Boris Yeltsin, emerged as the successor state.

The Legacy of the Soviet Union

The collapse of the Soviet Union marked the end of an era, but its legacy continues to shape the world. Many of the republics that were once part of the USSR, including Russia, Ukraine, and the Baltic States, continue to grapple with the political, economic, and social consequences of the Soviet era. The Soviet Union’s influence on global politics, military strategy, and culture remains profound, and its collapse marked the shift from a bipolar world to a more complex international system.

Legacy of the Soviet Union

The legacy of the Soviet Union is still felt across the globe. From the political and economic systems in Russia and Eastern Europe to the culture and ideology of the socialist movement, the impact of the Soviet Union continues to shape the world. Some view the USSR as a beacon of socialism and social justice, while others see it as a totalitarian state responsible for repression, war, and the suppression of human rights.

Political Influence

Many former Soviet republics, including Russia, still maintain a strong relationship with the legacy of Soviet political ideologies. In countries such as Belarus, the Communist Party still holds significant power, and political figures like Vladimir Putin in Russia have invoked Soviet symbols and rhetoric to consolidate their power. The continued prominence of these Soviet-inspired ideologies serves as a reminder of the USSR's lasting influence on global political thought.

Cultural Influence

The Soviet Union’s cultural legacy remains evident in the art, literature, and cinema of the former Soviet states. Soviet artists, writers, and filmmakers continue to inspire new generations, and the themes of socialism and collectivism still resonate in the works produced in the region. Even in the West, many admire the achievements of Soviet cinema, ballet, and visual arts.

Economic Systems

While the Soviet economic model is no longer in place, it has influenced the development of socialist economies worldwide. The ideas of central planning, collective ownership, and state control over industry continue to shape the economic systems of countries such as Cuba, China, and North Korea.

The Cold War: A Battle for Global Supremacy

The Cold War was a period of intense political and military rivalry between the United States and its allies, and the Soviet Union and its satellite states. This ideological, geopolitical, and military conflict lasted from the end of World War II in 1945 until the collapse of the Soviet Union in 1991. The Cold War was characterized by competition for global influence, nuclear arms races, proxy wars, and espionage, yet it stopped short of direct military confrontation between the two superpowers.

The Beginnings of the Cold War

The Cold War began in the aftermath of World War II, as the world emerged from the devastation of the conflict. The Soviet Union, led by Joseph Stalin, sought to expand its influence in Eastern Europe and spread communism across the globe. In contrast, the United States, led by President Harry S. Truman, and its Western allies were determined to stop the spread of communism, promoting capitalism and democracy as alternatives.

The ideological divide between the Soviet Union and the United States created an intense rivalry. The Soviet Union’s actions in Eastern Europe, including the imposition of communist governments in countries such as Poland, Hungary, and Czechoslovakia, were seen as a direct challenge to Western democratic values. The U.S. responded by implementing the Truman Doctrine, a policy of containing communism and providing military and economic assistance to countries resisting Soviet influence.

Key Events of the Cold War

The Cold War was marked by numerous crises and confrontations between the Soviet Union and the United States, including:

  • The Berlin Airlift (1948–1949): After the Soviet Union blockaded West Berlin in an attempt to force the Allies out of the city, the United States and its allies responded by organizing a massive airlift to supply West Berlin with food, fuel, and other necessities.
  • The Cuban Missile Crisis (1962): One of the most dangerous moments of the Cold War, the Cuban Missile Crisis brought the world to the brink of nuclear war. The Soviet Union placed nuclear missiles in Cuba, leading to a tense standoff with the U.S. President John F. Kennedy. After intense negotiations, the crisis was resolved, and the Soviet Union agreed to remove the missiles.
  • The Korean War (1950–1953): The Korean War was a proxy conflict in which communist North Korea, supported by China and the Soviet Union, fought against South Korea, supported by the United States and other UN nations. The war ended in a stalemate, with Korea remaining divided along the 38th parallel.
  • The Vietnam War (1955–1975): The Vietnam War was another proxy conflict in which the Soviet Union supported North Vietnam, while the United States backed South Vietnam. The war ended in 1975 with the fall of Saigon and the reunification of Vietnam under communist rule.

The Arms Race

One of the defining features of the Cold War was the nuclear arms race. Both the Soviet Union and the United States sought to build up massive nuclear arsenals, each side fearing the other's capabilities. The development of the hydrogen bomb by both superpowers in the 1950s marked a new phase in the arms race. The Soviet Union and the United States tested increasingly powerful nuclear weapons, and both sides maintained a policy of mutually assured destruction (MAD), wherein both countries were capable of annihilating each other in the event of a nuclear war.

The arms race led to the establishment of arms control agreements, such as the Partial Test Ban Treaty (1963), the Strategic Arms Limitation Talks (SALT I and SALT II), and the Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces (INF) Treaty, which sought to limit the proliferation of nuclear weapons. However, the threat of nuclear war remained a constant presence throughout the Cold War period.

Space Race

The Cold War also saw intense competition in space exploration. The Soviet Union and the United States vied for dominance in space, which was seen as a measure of technological and ideological superiority. In 1957, the Soviet Union launched Sputnik 1, the first artificial satellite, marking the beginning of the space race. This event shocked the world and led to significant investments in science and technology, particularly in the United States.

In 1961, Soviet cosmonaut Yuri Gagarin became the first human to travel into space, further heightening tensions between the superpowers. In response, President John F. Kennedy announced the goal of landing a man on the moon, and in 1969, American astronaut Neil Armstrong became the first person to walk on the moon. The space race was a major aspect of Cold War rivalry, symbolizing both the technological prowess and political ideologies of the superpowers.

The Détente Era

In the 1970s, the Cold War entered a period known as détente, marked by a relaxation of tensions between the superpowers. This was a time of increased diplomatic engagement and arms control agreements. Leaders like U.S. President Richard Nixon and Soviet Premier Leonid Brezhnev sought to ease the tensions of the Cold War through a series of summits and treaties.

The most notable of these was the Strategic Arms Limitation Talks (SALT I), which led to the first agreements to limit the number of nuclear weapons held by both sides. Despite the progress made during this period, the underlying ideological conflict between the superpowers remained, and tensions would resurge in the 1980s.

The End of the Cold War

The Cold War came to an end in the late 1980s and early 1990s, largely due to the efforts of Soviet leader Mikhail Gorbachev. Gorbachev introduced a series of reforms, including glasnost (openness) and perestroika (restructuring), which aimed to address the Soviet Union’s political and economic problems. Gorbachev’s willingness to engage with the West and reduce the Soviet military presence in Eastern Europe paved the way for the peaceful end of the Cold War.

The fall of the Berlin Wall in 1989 symbolized the collapse of communism in Eastern Europe, and by 1991, the Soviet Union itself dissolved, marking the official end of the Cold War. The United States emerged as the world’s sole superpower, and the global political landscape shifted dramatically.

The Soviet Union’s Influence on Modern Politics

The legacy of the Soviet Union continues to shape political and ideological debates around the world. While the Soviet Union no longer exists, the political and economic systems it left behind still influence contemporary discussions about socialism, capitalism, and the role of the state in society.

Socialism and Marxist Ideology

The Soviet Union was the largest experiment in socialism in the 20th century, and its ideological foundations were rooted in the works of Karl Marx and Vladimir Lenin. The Soviet system promoted the idea of a classless society in which the means of production were owned collectively by the state. While the collapse of the USSR marked the end of this particular experiment in socialism, the ideas of Marxism-Leninism continue to inspire political movements around the world, particularly in countries like Cuba, China, and North Korea.

Modern-day socialist parties and movements often invoke the ideals of the Soviet Union, though they may not advocate for the totalitarian methods used by the USSR. Many leftist movements view the Soviet Union as an important symbol of resistance to imperialism and capitalism, even though they criticize the authoritarian nature of the regime.

Global Influence and Legacy

In addition to its ideological influence, the Soviet Union had a profound impact on global politics. The USSR was a major player in the creation of international organizations such as the United Nations and the Warsaw Pact, and it played a central role in the post-World War II order. The Soviet Union’s support for anti-colonial movements and revolutionary governments in Africa, Asia, and Latin America helped spread communist ideals to various parts of the world.

In the post-Soviet era, Russia has sought to reclaim some of its former influence on the world stage. Under the leadership of Vladimir Putin, Russia has reasserted itself as a major global power, particularly in Eastern Europe and the Middle East. The legacy of the Soviet Union is still felt in Russian foreign policy and in the way Russia interacts with former Soviet republics and the broader international community.
